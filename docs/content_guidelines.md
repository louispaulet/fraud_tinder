# Content Guidelines

Fraud Tinder uses fictional, synthetic examples only. The goal is to teach scam-detection patterns without copying real victims, real listings, private messages, or marketplace assets.

## Allowed Content

Use fictional examples such as:

- Fake marketplace listings.
- Fictional article previews.
- Fictional SMS or email-style messages.
- Synthetic product illustrations.
- Styled placeholders.
- Fictional receipts, invoices, and notices.
- Invented seller names, publication names, vendors, and dossier IDs.

## Disallowed Content

Do not use:

- Real eBay listings.
- Real marketplace screenshots.
- Real scam screenshots.
- Real private messages.
- Real victim stories presented as game content.
- Copied third-party brand assets.
- Logos from real companies unless there is a clear legal/product reason and the user explicitly asks for it.

## Scam Case Design

Good fictional scam cases should include a few recognizable risk signals:

- Extreme urgency.
- Off-platform payment requests.
- Unusually low prices.
- Generic sender identity.
- Pressure to act before verifying.
- Requests for card details, credentials, or codes.
- Suspicious delivery or refund fees.
- Mismatched seller history and item value.

Avoid making every scam cartoonishly obvious. The game is more useful when players must weigh multiple clues.

## Not-Scam Case Design

Legitimate cases should be plausible, not perfect.

They can include ordinary imperfections:

- Used-item wear.
- Modest seller quirks.
- Normal delays.
- Small deposits.
- Reprints or refurbished items when clearly disclosed.

The explanation should clarify why the case is acceptable without teaching players that any one signal is always safe.

## Explanation Style

After each decision, explanations should be:

- Short.
- Specific to the case.
- Focused on observable clues.
- Non-shaming if the player got it wrong.

Prefer: "The off-platform payment request removes buyer protection."

Avoid: "Only careless people fall for this."
